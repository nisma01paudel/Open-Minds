# Slope / Landslide Hazard Responsibility Map — Nepal

**Artifact A: authority-routing ontology (asset × location × hazard) → named institution + mandate citation**

Compiled 2026-09-30. Legal research from primary texts (statutes, official departmental pages) plus official government reports. Every row carries a source. Anything not verified is marked **UNCERTAIN** — no mandate has been invented.

## Method and honesty statement

- Primary legal texts were downloaded and read directly, not summarised second-hand: Constitution of Nepal 2072 (English, 1st Amendment); Local Government Operation Act 2074 in **both** the enacted Nepali (FAOLEX NEP209136) and an English translation (ADB LPR); DRRM Act 2074 (English, FAOLEX NEP228597); DRRM Rules 2076 (English, FAOLEX NEP228606).
- Section numbers were cross-checked between the enacted Nepali and the English translation. Where the two diverge (the English translation is of the *bill* stage), the enacted Nepali governs and this is stated.
- Grey literature is used only where it is the government's own publication (Nepal Disaster Report 2024) or a department's own review/annual report.
- **Confidence** = confidence that the named institution/office is the correct routing target for that class of decision, given the evidence. It is *not* confidence that the institution will act.

**Two ladders that are routinely confused**

- **Have the hazard fixed (development/maintenance duty):** Ward → Municipality (for local roads/slopes) → Road Division Office (national highway) → DWIDM division (river/landslide control works) → Province (provincial road) → NDRRMA/MoHA. This is a *capital and maintenance* chain and it has a budget cycle.
- **Respond now (emergency duty):** Ward committee → LDMC (Mayor) → DDMC (CDO) → Provincial committee → NDRRMA → National Council. This is an *emergency* chain and it is triggered by an event.

A blocked road sits in **both**. Emergency clearance is the LDMC/DDMC's; permanent repair is the road owner's.

## 3. Is there an existing authoritative "who is responsible" register?

**Short answer: No — not one that routes a specific slope to a named office. But several partial registers exist, and we must cite them.**

What the Government of Nepal *does* publish:

1. **The Constitution's Schedules 5–9 and LGOA 2074 s.11** — a **subject-wise** allocation of powers between federation, province and local level. Verified text: Schedule 8 = local sole powers (incl. item 11 local/rural/agro roads, item 20 disaster management); Schedule 9 = concurrent all three tiers (incl. item 9 disaster management); Schedule 5 item 20 = national highways (federal); Schedule 6 item 12 = state highways (province); Schedule 7 item 17 = early preparedness/rescue/relief/rehabilitation from calamities (concurrent federation–province). **This allocates subjects, not assets, and never names an office.**
2. **The DRRM Act 2074 and DRRM Rules 2076** — a **tier-wise** committee architecture (National Council → Executive Committee → NDRRMA → provincial committee → DDMC → LDMC). Again: a *tier* register, not a hazard register.
3. **The National Disaster Response Framework (NDRF)** — created **10 humanitarian clusters** (health, WASH, emergency shelter, food security, nutrition, camp coordination and camp management, protection, early recovery, education, logistics, emergency communication), **led by respective line ministries and co-led by UN agencies/IFRC**. These clusters are **sectoral response coordination**, not hazard-specific development mandates, and **none is a "landslide" or "geology" cluster**. Reported in the Government of Nepal's own *Nepal Disaster Report 2024*.
4. **District Disaster Preparedness and Response Plans (DPRP)**, produced annually in each district. These **do** carry a table titled *"क्षेत्रगत अगुवा तथा सम्मिलित संरचना"* — **sector-wise lead agency, named contact person, phone number and email** — verified directly in the **Rasuwa DPRP 2079**. This is the closest thing to an operational responsibility register that exists. But it is **per-district, per-year, PDF, in Nepali, response-oriented (9 clusters: coordination/SAR, food & agriculture, emergency shelter, health & nutrition, education, protection & camp management, WASH, early recovery), and contains no landslide row.**
5. **BIPAD** (bipad.gov.np, Building Information Platform Against Disaster) — launched by MoHA and NDRRMA; a Disaster Information Management System. Per an independent assessment published through the Australian DFAT-funded OPML programme, *"In its current state, the BIPAD portal does not support data processing for risk analysis but is limited to compiling multiple disaster datasets and supporting visualizing risk information."* It is a **hazard, exposure, incident and loss/damage** platform. It is **not** a responsibility or mandate register.

**Conclusion to state in the paper:** Nepal publishes a **subject-wise** power allocation (Constitution/LGOA), a **tier-wise** committee architecture (DRRM Act/Rules), a **sectoral response cluster** framework at national level (NDRF) and a **district-level sectoral lead-agency table** (annual DPRPs). **It does not publish any asset × location × hazard → named office register, and none of the existing instruments carries a mandate citation per row.** Our contribution is therefore **not** "the first responsibility list for Nepal" — that claim would be false and is exactly what the NDRF and DPRPs occupy. Our defensible novelty is narrower and still real: **the first machine-readable, asset-anchored routing key that resolves to a specific office with the governing section cited, and that states its own gaps.**

## 4. Who is legally liable if a known-hazard slope is left unaddressed and later fails?

### 4.1 The statutory hook — DRRM Act 2074 s.24 (Offence and Punishment)

Read verbatim from the English text (FAOLEX NEP228597, p.78):

- **s.24(1)** — "Whoever commits negligence that may be a cause to occur any disaster, or directly involves to cause such incident, shall be punishable with **fine up to five lakh rupees or imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or both**, based on the severity of an offence."
- **s.24(3)** — "Without prejudice to the provisions of sub-section (1), where the offence committed thereunder has attributable to a **loss of life**, or sustained impairment to any part of body, or bodily injury, such a person shall be punishable **under the prevailing law**."
- **s.24(4)** — refusing to comply with orders or directions issued under the Act: fine up to **NPR 50,000**.
- **s.25** — compensation "may be recovered to every person who sustained losses or damages by way of offence committed by any person under this Act."
- **s.26** — a case must be filed within **30 days** of the offence.
- **s.27** — any case under the Act is a **state case**, deemed included in Schedule-1 of the State Cases Act 2049 (1993).
- **s.31** — no suit or prosecution lies against a civil servant or employee for anything done **in good faith** under the Act. **This is the practical shield that any accountability claim must get past.**

**Reading:** s.24(1) is a genuine negligence offence that reaches officials and bodies with a DRRM duty. s.24(3) routes cases involving death into the general criminal law. s.26's 30-day limitation is extremely short for a landslide, where causation and negligence typically take months to establish. **UNCERTAIN:** I could not verify a single instance of an individual being convicted under DRRM Act s.24 for a fatal landslide.

### 4.2 The demolition/compulsion route

**LGOA 2074 s.41**: if the owner does not demolish within the appeal/order period, **the rural or urban municipality shall demolish it itself and recover the cost from the owner**. This is a real, enforceable municipal power — but it is written for **buildings and structures**, not for slopes. **UNCERTAIN:** no provision found that compels a private landowner to stabilise a bare slope.

### 4.3 The documented case — Simaltal, 12 July 2024 (Narayanghat–Muglin highway)

This is the strongest accountability precedent in the record and it cuts **across** levels of government.

A six-member government study task force (**कार्यदल**) convened by the **Ministry of Home Affairs**, coordinated by Home Ministry Joint Secretary **Chhabi Rijal**, submitted its report to the Home Minister on **6 August 2024**. Findings as reported by BBC Nepali:

- The **main cause** was a **rural road built on the steep hillside above the highway without proper engineering or design and without attention to environmental factors**.
- That road was constructed by **Bharatpur Metropolitan City** — the **Simaltal–Bangresal–Dumregau road**.
- Spoil excavated from the road was **dumped loose on the slope beside the road and never cleared**, so a large mass of rock and soil came down at once and blocked the highway.
- **Check dams and gabion walls built in the stream a few years earlier could not bear the load**, and the debris flow pushed the two buses into the Trishuli river.
- There was **no integrated road-safety information system**, so no one knew which roads were blocked and operators could not decide whether to dispatch buses.

Casualties: two buses, 62 passengers; 3 survivors from one bus; 19 bodies recovered and identified; **40 passengers and both buses never found**.

**Follow-through:** a year later, on **11 July 2025**, Kantipur's headline was *"Anniversary of Simaltal bus accident: The committee's recommendation is pending."* So the accountability finding exists and the implementation does not.

**Why this matters for the responsibility map:** the fatal slope failure was **caused by a local government's rural-road construction** and **killed people on a federal national highway**. This is precisely the case where a map that says "the highway is DoR's problem" would misroute responsibility. Any routing key must be able to name the *municipality* as the actor for the upslope road.

### 4.4 Other accountability leads (weaker)

- **Tikapur Municipality, Kailali** — Republica reported that Planning Branch Chief **Navraj Joshi** faced action for **unauthorised road repair**. I could not retrieve the article (HTTP 403), so this is recorded as a **lead, not a verified finding**.
- The **Commission for the Investigation of Abuse of Authority (CIAA)** and the **Office of the Auditor General** are the bodies that would carry road-maintenance and procurement accountability, but I did not verify a specific CIAA/OAG finding on unrepaired landslide-prone road sections. **UNCERTAIN.**
- A Supreme Court document reference (*भानु जोशी समेत*, 075-CR-0073, "अनियमितता") surfaced in search but I could not verify its subject matter. **UNCERTAIN.**

### 4.5 Practical answer to "who is liable"

- **Institutionally:** the tier holding the duty — municipality (local road, land-use, unsafe structure), DoR Road Division (national highway), DWIDM (river/landslide control works), province (provincial road).
- **Personally:** DRRM Act s.24(1) is available against a negligent official; s.24(3) escalates where there is loss of life; but **s.31's good-faith shield, s.26's 30-day limitation and the absence of any verified conviction make this a weak and slow route in practice.**
- **The realistic accountability mechanism in Nepal is not prosecution — it is inquiry report + suspension/transfer + political pressure**, as Simaltal shows. **State this plainly; do not promise a liability regime that does not operate.**

## 5. Practical reality check — does a ward chair act on an engineering warning?

### 5.1 What the government's own report says (strongest evidence)

From the Government of Nepal's **Nepal Disaster Report 2024** (NDRRMA / MoHA):

- *"Challenges, however, remain on: (a) effectively implementing action plans at the provincial and local levels primarily due to **inadequate technical and functional capacities**; and (b) **financing challenges** to invest adequately in DRM. In some cases, municipalities have either **not established disaster management committees** or established their local emergency operation centres or formed DM fund guidelines or set up DM fund account."*
- *"given the rugged topography and inaccessibility, local governments in hazard-prone areas are faced with **resource and capacity limitations, and are less equipped to undertake detailed risk assessments**, preparedness and response measures."*
- *"Although local governments have built institutional structures to effectively implement DRRM activities, they **often lack adequate equipment and trained human resources** to undertake DRRM. … The existing local government's DRRM capacity is **insufficient**."*
- Positively: *"By the end of 2023, **all 753 local governments have completed DRRM training via MoFAGA**"* — the delivery channel to every municipality exists and is used.
- The **CDO's performance contract** carries six indicators, one of which *"empowers CDOs with overall authority to preparedness for response and to manage search and rescue management at the district level"*, evaluated on the district preparedness plan, QRT mobilisation, DEOC operationalisation, search and rescue, relief distribution and coordination meetings. **This is the single most important lever in the whole system: routing an advisory through the CDO/DDMC attaches it to someone whose own appraisal depends on it.**

### 5.2 What independent and academic evidence says

- **ActionAid Nepal / DPNet-Nepal, *Decentralized Disaster Risk Governance in Nepal: Ground Reflections*:** *"The extent to which these roles have been fulfilled is a matter of concern."* Same study: *"Only seven municipalities which have prepared LDCRP have identified the areas and communities that are exposed to various hazards, using participatory tools."* Also records that local governments **could not spend allocated budgets** on capacity-building and preparedness, and notes **limited budget** and **lack of technical capacity** among the constraints.
- **Lund University study, three municipalities in Karnali province** (70 surveys, 44 interviews, 3 FGDs with municipal staff): disaster risk information access relies heavily on **in-community sources and in-person communication**; *"The reach of institutions is limited, with their usage decreasing with increasing remoteness"*; gaps are driven by *"technological challenges, misalignment of municipalities to local priorities and capacities and challenges associated with risk perception."* Residents *"stress the desire for more guidance on preparedness, particularly from local institutions."*
- **Tribhuvan University thesis, Godawari Municipality** (ward-chair interviews included): ward-level analysis was needed because exposure differs sharply by ward (Ward 7 highest landslide exposure); *"there's basically no early warning system that works (43%)"*; budget named as a principal constraint.
- **Study of rural mountainous Nepal (NepJOL)**: *"A shortage of trained engineers and masons in rural areas"*; *"Municipalities cannot often monitor compliance"* with building standards.
- **Practical Action Nepal (May 2025)** developed rainfall intensity–duration thresholds for Helambu–Panchpokhari that were **integrated into DHM's operational forecasting system** — so a threshold-to-warning pipeline already exists for at least one slope cluster.

### 5.3 What this implies for an advisory to be actionable — and where I am honest about the gap

What the evidence **supports**:

1. **Address it to the LDMC/Mayor, and copy the DDMC/CDO.** The CDO's performance contract is the system's strongest incentive, and the district DPRP already has a named lead-agency table to slot into.
2. **Route it through an existing plan instrument, not around it.** Districts plan annually (DPRP) and municipalities have LDCRPs; an advisory that arrives as an input to a plan cycle has a budget line to attach to. Only a minority of municipalities have a hazard-identified LDCRP — so the advisory may have to help create that identification rather than assume it.
3. **Name the ward and the asset, and state the consequence in loss terms.** Ward-level exposure varies enormously (Godawari: Ward 7 highest landslide exposure), so a municipality-wide message is not actionable but a Ward-No.-Y message is.
4. **Do not assume technical staff.** "Shortage of trained engineers"; "less equipped to undertake detailed risk assessments". The advisory must be readable by a non-engineer, in Nepali, and must say what to do, not only what is wrong.
5. **Expect the budget to be the binding constraint, not the belief.** Multiple independent sources name budget, not disbelief, as the central constraint. An advisory that arrives without a funding route (municipal DRM fund, provincial/ federal transfer, Road Board, DWIDM) is likely to be filed.

**What I could NOT establish — mark as UNCERTAIN:**

- I found **no study that directly tests whether a Nepali ward chair acts on a written engineering warning about a slope.** The evidence above is about capacity, budget, information access and plan quality — it is adjacent, not direct.
- I found **no published evidence** on whether a written request from a higher tier (DoR division, DMG, province) changes a municipality's behaviour.
- I found **no verified impact evaluation of impact-based forecasting or last-mile warning** in Nepal showing a behavioural effect at ward level.
- The practical claim "a written advisory becomes actionable when it is addressed to the LDMC, copied to the CDO, ward-specific, in Nepali, consequence-quantified and budget-linked" is therefore a **reasoned synthesis of adjacent evidence, not a verified finding.** The paper must label it as such, and testing it is a legitimate piece of our own contribution.
